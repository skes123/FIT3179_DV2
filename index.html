Meet the wildlife

From whales and dolphins to turtles, seals and sharks, Australia’s marine wildlife spans very different sizes and ways of life. This visualisation explores 20 selected species through their characteristics, recorded locations and conservation evidence.

Start by comparing marine mammals’ sizes, then explore observation patterns for all five wildlife groups. Finally, examine their reported threats and global conservation status.

Size varies widely among these marine mammals. Hover over the bars to explore their published adult lengths and source references. “Both” indicates an estimate reported for both sexes together. These values describe the species generally, rather than measurements taken from the Australian observation records.

These five species have separate published male and female mass estimates. Connected dots show the difference between those estimates, rather than the range of weights within each species. The mass axis uses a logarithmic scale: each labelled step represents a tenfold increase, so wider gaps indicate larger mass ratios. Among these five species, the southern elephant seal shows the largest male-to-female mass ratio: its published male estimate is about 5.5 times the female estimate.

Each point pairs published length and mass estimates for the same species and reported sex. These are species-level estimates, not measurements of individual animals. Hover to identify each point.

Where wildlife is recorded

Explore human observation records for our 20 selected species around Australia from 2015 to 2026. Recording effort varies between locations, so more records do not necessarily mean more animals.

Each coloured cell shows how many of our 20 selected species have human observation records there. Cells cover one degree of latitude and longitude. Blank areas have no records in this selection; they do not necessarily lack marine wildlife.

Circle area represents the number of human observation records in each one-degree cell. Larger circles indicate more recording activity. These totals are influenced by survey effort and do not measure animal abundance.

Patterns in the records

When are these species recorded? Explore monthly and yearly patterns in the human observation records. These patterns also reflect when and where people collect data.

Darker cells show months containing a larger share of that species’ records. Percentages are calculated separately for each species, making their recording patterns easier to compare despite different totals. Survey timing affects these patterns, and 2026 is incomplete. The chart alone cannot establish migration patterns.

Recorded observations rose from 1,185 in 2015 to 5,742 in 2025, nearly a fivefold increase. This reflects recording activity, which is influenced by survey effort and data availability; it does not demonstrate population growth. The 2026 total covers only part of the year.

Conservation and threats

Conservation status describes a species’ assessed risk of extinction. Global assessments can differ from Australian legal listings and from assessments of individual regional populations.

The five selected threat types are sea surface temperature anomalies, plastic, shipping, noise pollution and commercial fishing. This chart counts only entries marked “X”, meaning a current threat in the historical IUCN assessment cited by MegaMove. Other evidence codes are excluded. Zero means none of these five threats was marked X; it does not mean the species faces no threats. Counts describe documented threat types, not their severity.

In this selection, 13 of the 20 species (65%) fall into threatened categories: Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered. This percentage describes our selected species, not all Australian marine wildlife. Data Deficient means there is insufficient information to assess extinction risk.

The MegaMove categories include two confirmed updates: green turtle to Least Concern in 2025, and southern elephant seal to Vulnerable in IUCN’s April 2026 announcement. Global categories can conceal regional declines and differ from Australian legal protection.

Each tile represents one species, giving every species equal weight. Trends come from assessments cited in MegaMove, with updates from IUCN’s 2025 green turtle and 2026 southern elephant seal announcements. Evidence dates differ between species; hover to see them. Unknown means the population trend was not established. These are global trends and may differ from Australian populations.

A diverse ocean, an uneven picture

These 20 species reveal contrasting body sizes, recording patterns and conservation concerns. Observation maps show where evidence has been collected, while conservation assessments help explain the risks species face. Reading both together highlights the importance of continued monitoring and protection.
